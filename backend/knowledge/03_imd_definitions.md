# Meteorological definitions used in India

## Rainfall intensity categories (IMD, 24-hour totals)
- Light rain: 2.5–15.5 mm
- Moderate rain: 15.6–64.4 mm
- Heavy rain: 64.5–115.5 mm
- Very heavy rain: 115.6–204.4 mm
- Extremely heavy rain: 204.5 mm or more

## Cloudburst
IMD describes a cloudburst as an extreme rainfall event of about 100 mm or more in one hour over a small geographical area (roughly 20–30 km²). Cloudbursts are most common in hilly terrain, where moist air is forced up steep slopes. BUMBLEBLE's cloudburst product is the probability that the next hour's rainfall reaches 100 mm.

## Squall
IMD defines a squall as a sudden increase of wind speed by at least 3 stages on the Beaufort scale, with the speed rising to force 6 or more (about 22 knots / 40 km/h or more) and lasting at least one minute.

## Thunderstorm and dust storm
- A thunderstorm is a storm with lightning and thunder, usually with gusty winds and heavy rain, and sometimes hail.
- Pre-monsoon (March–May) thunderstorms over East and North-East India are called **Nor'westers** or **Kalbaisakhi**. They typically form over the Chota Nagpur plateau and move south-east towards the Gangetic plains, Kolkata, Odisha and Bangladesh, often as squall lines with damaging winds and hail.
- **Andhi** refers to dust storms and dust-raising thunderstorms over North-West India (Rajasthan, Punjab, Haryana, Delhi).

## IMD colour-coded warnings
IMD uses colour codes for impact-based warnings:
- **Green**: no warning, no action.
- **Yellow**: be updated (watch).
- **Orange**: be prepared (alert).
- **Red**: take action (warning).
BUMBLEBLE's Low / Moderate / High / Extreme threat colours are intentionally similar but are automated guidance, not official IMD warnings.

## Radar reflectivity guide (dBZ)
- 15–30 dBZ: light rain
- 30–40 dBZ: moderate rain
- 40–50 dBZ: heavy rain, thunderstorm cores
- 50–60 dBZ: very heavy rain, possibly small hail
- above 60 dBZ: intense convection, with a high likelihood of hail

## Nowcasting
Nowcasting means forecasting the weather in detail for the next 0–6 hours. It is mainly done by extrapolating current observations (radar, satellite, lightning) rather than by running a full numerical weather model. It is the standard technique for short-lived, localised severe weather such as thunderstorms.
